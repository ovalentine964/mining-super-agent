## 3. KENYA MINING ACT 2016 — KEY PROVISIONS ANALYSIS

### 3.1 Ownership and Control (Part II)

**Section 6 — Ownership of Minerals:**
> "(1) Every mineral — (a) in its natural state in, under or upon land in Kenya; (b) in or under a lake, river, stream, or water courses in Kenya; (c) in the exclusive economic zone and an area covered by the territorial sea or continental shelf, is the property of the Republic and is vested in the national government in trust for the people of Kenya."

> "(2) Subsection (1) applies **despite any right or ownership of or by any person** in relation to any land in, on or under which any minerals are found."

**Implication for Nyatike:** Even though Valentine's family owns the surface land, the gold and copper beneath it belong to the Republic. HOWEVER — this means NO ONE (including Chinese companies) can extract those minerals without authorization from the national government. The landowner's consent for surface access is still required.

### 3.2 Prohibition on Unauthorized Mining (Part V)

**Section 11 — Prohibition of unauthorized prospecting or mining:**
No person shall prospect for, mine, or carry on any operations for the winning of minerals unless authorized under a mineral right granted under this Act.

**Section 200 — Offences relating to mineral rights:**
Any person who contravenes the provisions of this Act commits an offense.

**Section 202 — Offenses and penalties:**
Provides for criminal penalties including fines and imprisonment for unauthorized mining operations.

**Implication for Nyatike:** Chinese operators mining without a license are committing a criminal offense under Kenyan law. This is not merely a civil dispute — it is a matter for the Directorate of Criminal Investigations (DCI).

### 3.3 Licensing Requirements

The Mining Act 2016 establishes a comprehensive licensing regime:

| License Type | Section | Eligibility | Area Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reconnaissance Licence | s.61 | Any person | Large area |
| Prospecting Licence | s.65-77 | Any person/company | Specific blocks |
| Retention Licence | s.78-90 | Holder of prospecting licence | Specific area |
| Mining Licence (Large Scale) | s.98-116 | Any person/company | Up to 50 grid units |
| Mining Permit (Small Scale) | s.118-130 | Kenyan citizen/company | Up to 10 hectares |
| Artisanal Mining Permit | s.92-97 | Kenyan citizen, 18+ | Small area |

**Key requirement:** ALL mineral rights require:
1. Application to the Mining Cadastre
2. Approval by the Mineral Rights Board
3. EIA license from NEMA
4. Community consultation (FPIC)
5. Payment of prescribed fees

### 3.4 Community Development Agreement (Section 172)

**Section 172** requires holders of large-scale mining licences to enter into Community Development Agreements (CDAs) with affected communities. This includes:
- Benefit-sharing arrangements
- Employment priorities for local communities
- Infrastructure development commitments
- Environmental protection measures

**Implication:** Even if a mining company obtains a valid license, they must negotiate a CDA with the community. Without this agreement, operations cannot proceed.

## 4. CONSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORK — LAND AND MINERAL RIGHTS

The Constitution of Kenya 2010 is the supreme law and provides the STRONGEST legal foundation for community resistance.

### 4.1 Key Constitutional Provisions

| Article | Provision | Relevance to Nyatike |
|---|---|---|
| **Article 10** | National values and principles of governance including **participation of the people**, **good governance**, **transparency**, **accountability** | Any mining without public participation is unconstitutional |
| **Article 26** | Right to life | Environmental degradation threatens life |
| **Article 27** | Right to equality and freedom from discrimination | Kenyans evicted while foreigners mine = discrimination |
| **Article 28** | Right to dignity | Forced displacement, pollution = dignity violation |
| **Article 40** | Right to property | Land cannot be compulsorily taken without compensation |
| **Article 42** | Right to a **clean and healthy environment** | Mining pollution violates this right |
| **Article 43** | Economic and social rights — right to food, water, health | Mining contamination threatens these rights |
| **Article 56** | Rights of minorities and marginalized groups | Mining communities qualify for special protection |
| **Article 60** | Principles of land policy — **equitable access**, **sustainable management**, **transparent land administration** | Land grabbing for mining violates these principles |
| **Article 62(1)(f)** | Public land includes all minerals and mineral oils | Minerals are public property held in trust |
| **Article 63(2)** | Community land held by communities identified on basis of ethnicity, culture, or similar community of interest | Nyatike community has collective land rights |
| **Article 66(2)** | The State may regulate the use of any land, or interest in, or right over, any land in the interest of **defence, public safety, public order, public morality, public health, or land use planning** | State can regulate mining for public interest |
| **Article 69(1)** | Obligations in respect of the environment including **ensure sustainable exploitation**, **eliminate processes likely to endanger the environment**, **utilize the environment for the benefit of all Kenyans** | Mining without environmental safeguards violates this |

| **Article 71** | Agreements relating to natural resources — any transaction must be ratified by Parliament | Secret deals with Chinese companies may be unconstitutional |

### 4.2 Constitutional Supremacy

The Constitution is supreme (Article 2(1)). Any law, practice, or act inconsistent with the Constitution is void. This means:
- Even if a corrupt politician "approves" mining, if it violates constitutional provisions (no FPIC, no EIA, environmental degradation), it is **unconstitutional and void**
- Community members can petition the High Court directly under Article 22 for violation of fundamental rights
- The Environment and Land Court has jurisdiction over these matters (Article 162(2)(b))

## 5. FPIC (FREE, PRIOR, INFORMED CONSENT) REQUIREMENTS

### 5.1 What is FPIC?

FPIC is an international legal standard that requires:
- **Free:** Consent given without coercion, intimidation, or manipulation
- **Prior:** Consent sought BEFORE any mining activities commence
- **Informed:** Full disclosure of project scope, impacts, risks, and benefits
- **Consent:** The right to say NO — consent can be withheld

### 5.2 FPIC in Kenyan Law

FPIC is NOT a single statutory provision but is embedded across multiple legal frameworks:

| Source | FPIC Provision |
|---|---|
| **Constitution Article 10** | Participation of the people — public participation is a national value |
| **Constitution Article 60** | Equitable access to land — requires community involvement |
| **Constitution Article 69(1)(a)** | Ensure sustainable exploitation of natural resources — implies community input |
| **Mining Act 2016 — Section 172** | Community Development Agreement requirement for large-scale mining |
| **Mining Act 2016 — Mining Regulations** | Community consultation requirements during licensing |
| **EMCA 1999 — Section 58** | EIA process requires public participation |
| **Land Act 2012 — Section 7** | Community participation in land management |
| **Community Land Act 2016** | Consent of community required for use of community land |
| **UNDRIP (UN Declaration on Rights of Indigenous Peoples)** | Article 32 — FPIC for resource extraction on indigenous lands |
| **ILO Convention 169** | Kenya is NOT a signatory, but the principles influence jurisprudence |
| **African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights** | Article 21 — right to freely dispose of natural resources |

### 5.3 FPIC Practical Requirements

For a mining operation to meet FPIC standards in Kenya:

1. **Disclosure:** The company must provide complete information about:
   - Nature and scale of mining operations
   - Environmental impact assessment results
   - Chemicals to be used (especially relevant for leaching operations)
   - Duration of operations
   - Employment and benefit-sharing plans
   - Remediation and closure plans

2. **Consultation:** The company must:
   - Hold public barazas (community meetings) in the local language
   - Provide adequate notice (not 24 hours)
   - Allow questions and objections
   - Document all consultations

3. **Consent:** The community must:
   - Be represented by legitimate leaders
   - Have access to independent legal and technical advice
   - Have the right to refuse consent
   - Not be subjected to bribery, threats, or manipulation

4. **Ongoing Consent:** FPIC is not a one-time event. Communities can withdraw consent if:
   - Operations deviate from what was described
   - Environmental damage exceeds projections
   - Benefits promised are not delivered
   - New information emerges about risks

### 5.4 FPIC Violations in Nyatike

Based on the context provided, the Chinese operators have likely violated FPIC in EVERY dimension:

| FPIC Requirement | Status in Nyatike |
|---|---|
| Free (no coercion) | ❌ **VIOLATED** — Operators working through corrupt politicians |
| Prior (before operations) | ❌ **VIOLATED** — Operations began without consultation |
| Informed (full disclosure) | ❌ **VIOLATED** — Community not informed about leaching chemicals, environmental risks |
| Consent (right to refuse) | ❌ **VIOLATED** — Community not asked; 1M KSH offers are coercive |

## 6. LANDOWNER RIGHTS AGAINST UNAUTHORIZED MINING

### 6.1 What Landowners CAN Do

| Right | Legal Basis | How to Exercise |
|---|---|---|
| **Refuse surface access** | Property rights (Constitution Art. 40) | Deny physical entry to land |
| **Demand to see license** | Mining Act s.11, s.196 | Ask miners for their mineral right documentation |
| **Report unauthorized mining** | Mining Act s.200-202 | File complaint with DCI, Mines Department |
| **File constitutional petition** | Constitution Art. 22, 70 | Petition Environment and Land Court |
| **Seek injunction** | Civil Procedure Rules | Court order to stop operations immediately |
| **Claim compensation** | Constitution Art. 40, Land Act | Sue for environmental damage, loss of use |
| **Report environmental crimes** | EMCA s.137-146 | File complaint with NEMA |
| **Report to anti-corruption** | Anti-Corruption and Economic Crimes Act | Report to EACC |

### 6.2 What Landowners CANNOT Do

| Action | Why Not |
|---|---|
| Claim ownership of minerals | Section 6 — minerals vest in national government |
| Mine without a license | Section 11 — even on your own land |
| Physically assault miners | Criminal offense — use legal channels instead |
| Extort or demand bribes from miners | Criminal offense |
| Block lawful mining operations with valid license | Could face contempt or obstruction charges |

### 6.3 The Critical Distinction

**Landowners own the SURFACE. The STATE owns the MINERALS.**

This means:
- Chinese companies need BOTH: (1) a mining license from the state AND (2) surface access consent from the landowner
- If they have a mining license but no landowner consent, they must apply to the National Land Commission for compulsory acquisition (with compensation)
- If they have NEITHER, they are trespassing AND committing criminal offenses under the Mining Act

## 7. CAN A COMMUNITY BLOCK MINING? UNDER WHAT CONDITIONS?

### 7.1 YES — Communities Can Block Mining When:

| Condition | Legal Basis | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **No mining license** | Mining Act s.11 | Report to DCI for criminal prosecution |
| **No EIA license** | EMCA s.58 | Report to NEMA for enforcement |
| **No FPIC** | Constitution Art. 10, 60, 69 | Constitutional petition |
| **No Community Development Agreement** | Mining Act s.172 | Refuse to negotiate; report non-compliance |
| **Environmental degradation** | Constitution Art. 42, 69 | Petition Environment and Land Court |
| **Health risks** | Constitution Art. 43 | Emergency injunction application |
| **Corruption in approval process** | Anti-Corruption Act | Report to EACC; challenge validity of approvals |
| **Foreign nationals without work permits** | Immigration Act | Report to Immigration Department |
| **Operations on community land** | Community Land Act 2016 | Community land rights claim |

### 7.2 Community Legal Strategy — Phased Approach

**Phase 1: DOCUMENTATION (Week 1-2)**
- Photograph all mining activities, machinery, workers
- Document environmental damage (polluted water, destroyed vegetation, excavations)
- Record names/descriptions of Chinese operators
- Document any payments/bribes to local politicians
- Collect water samples from affected streams/rivers
- Get GPS coordinates of mining sites

**Phase 2: FORMAL COMPLAINTS (Week 2-4)**
- File criminal complaint with DCI (unauthorized mining)
- File environmental complaint with NEMA (no EIA, pollution)
- Report to County Director of Mines (no license)
- Report to Immigration Department (foreign workers)
- Report to EACC (corruption allegations)
- File complaint with National Land Commission

**Phase 3: LEGAL ACTION (Week 4-8)**
- File constitutional petition in Environment and Land Court
- Seek conservatory order (interim injunction)
- Engage media for public pressure
- Contact human rights organizations (KHRC, ICJ-Kenya)

**Phase 4: ENFORCEMENT (Ongoing)**
- Monitor compliance with court orders
- Document any violations
- Report non-compliance to court
- Escalate to higher courts if necessary

### 7.3 Obstacles and Countermeasures

| Obstacle | Countermeasure |
|---|---|
| Corrupt local officials bypass community | File complaints directly to NATIONAL agencies (DCI HQ, NEMA HQ, Mines HQ) |
| Politicians threaten community members | Document threats; report to KNHRC; seek police protection |
| Company claims to have license | Demand to SEE the license; verify with Mining Cadastre |
| Company claims community consent | Demand proof of FPIC process; challenge validity |
| Court delays | Seek conservatory/interim orders for immediate relief |
| Lack of legal representation | Contact legal aid organizations (FIDA, Kituo Cha Sheria, ICJ-Kenya) |
| Intimidation by company-hired security | Report to police; document; seek court protection order |

## 9. CASE LAW: TOM MBOYA ODEGE V CABINET SECRETARY (2019)

### 9.1 Case Summary

**Citation:** Tom Mboya Odege v Cabinet Secretary, Ministry of Petroleum and Mining & 3 others [2019] KEELC 4618 (KLR)  
**Court:** Environment and Land Court at Migori  
**Case Number:** ELC Petition Case No. 2 of 2018  
**Judge:** GMA Ongondo  
**Date:** 15 January 2019

### 9.2 Facts

- Hon. Tom Mboya Odege, MP for Nyatike Constituency, filed a petition against the Cabinet Secretary for Mining, Director of Mines, and **Lijin Mining Company Limited** (a Chinese company)
- Lijin Mining's directors were Chinese nationals
- The company was engaged in illegal mining and prospecting of gold and copper in **Macalder area, Nyatike Constituency, Migori County**
- The mining was on trust land within Nyatike
- **No public participation** was conducted before commencement
- **No consents or approvals** were obtained from Migori County Government, National Land Commission, National Government, or Kenya Forest Service
- Environmental degradation including pollution and effects on human health resulted

### 9.3 Key Legal Claims

The petitioner sought declarations that:
1. Rights to life (Art. 26), dignity (Art. 28), and economic/social rights (Art. 43) of Nyatike residents were contravened
2. Foreign nationals were allowed to undertake illegal mining **to the exclusion of residents**
3. Mining activities without licences violated the Constitution and Mining Act 2016
4. Discrimination against Nyatike constituents (Art. 27, 56)

### 9.4 Critical Evidence — Government's Own Admission

**The Director of Mines (2nd respondent) SUPPORTED the petition:**
> "The respondent never issued a license or permit to the 3rd respondent to undertake any mining activity... officials found the 3rd respondent carrying out mining activities without any licence or permit. They immediately stopped the 3rd respondent who **ignored the stop order** and continued with illegal mining operations."

The Director confirmed the mining activities were in contravention of **Sections 11, 200, and 202 of the Mining Act, 2016**.

### 9.5 Relevance to Current Situation

This case is DIRECTLY on point:
- Same location (Nyatike, Macalder)
- Same type of actors (Chinese nationals)
- Same minerals (gold and copper)
- Same violations (no license, no FPIC, no EIA)
- Court confirmed illegality
- Government admitted no license was issued

**This precedent should be cited in any legal action against current Chinese operators.**

### 11.2 Whistleblower Protections

The **Witness Protection Act, 2006** provides protections for persons who report:
- Criminal offenses (including illegal mining)
- Corruption
- Environmental crimes

**Key protections include:**
- Identity confidentiality
- Police protection
- Relocation if necessary
- Non-disclosure of witness identity

### 11.4 Protection Against Retaliation

Community members who resist mining operations are protected against:
- **Physical violence:** Criminal law applies — assault, threats are offenses
- **Economic retaliation:** Cannot be fired from legitimate employment for exercising constitutional rights
- **Arbitrary arrest:** Police cannot arrest without reasonable suspicion of an offense
- **Land grabbing:** Compulsory acquisition requires due process and compensation (Land Act 2012)
- **Defamation:** Truth is a defense; public interest reporting is protected

**If retaliation occurs:**
1. Document everything (dates, times, witnesses, evidence)
2. Report to police (get an OB number)
3. Report to KNCHR
4. Contact legal aid organizations
5. Media exposure (retaliators often back down when publicized)

## 14. CONCLUSION

### 14.1 The Community Has Strong Legal Grounds

The Nyatike community is NOT powerless against Chinese mining operators. Kenyan law provides multiple layers of protection:

1. **Criminal law** — Unauthorized mining is a criminal offense punishable by fines and imprisonment
2. **Constitutional law** — Fundamental rights to property, clean environment, and dignity cannot be violated
3. **Environmental law** — NEMA has power to stop operations and order cleanup
4. **Mining law** — Comprehensive licensing system exists; violators face penalties
5. **Anti-corruption law** — Corrupt politicians can be prosecuted
6. **Immigration law** — Foreign nationals without work permits can be deported

- Accurate section numbers

- Document generators
- Community education materials

6. **Persistence** — Legal processes take time; don't give up

- Communities CAN sue and WIN against illegal Chinese miners in Nyatike
