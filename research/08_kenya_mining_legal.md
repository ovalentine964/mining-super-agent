## 1. KENYA MINING ACT 2016 — KEY PROVISIONS

### 1.1 Ownership of Minerals

**CRITICAL FACT: You do NOT own the minerals on your land.**

Under the Mining Act, 2016 (Cap 306), Section 4 and related provisions:

- **All minerals** in Kenya — whether on land, in water bodies, lakes, rivers, or within Kenya's exclusive economic zones — are **vested in the national government** in trust for the people of Kenya
- This means even minerals found on **private land** belong to the **state**, not the landowner
- The government has a **right of pre-emption** over all strategic minerals
- This is a constitutional provision under Articles 60, 62(1)(f), 66(2), 69, and 71 of the Constitution of Kenya 2010

**What this means for Valentine:** His family does NOT automatically own the gold under their land. However, they have critical **consent rights** and **priority access** that must be respected.

### 1.2 Landowner Rights Under the Mining Act

Despite not owning the minerals, landowners have important protections:

- **Consent Requirement (Section 40):** Mining on private or community land **requires the owner's express consent**. No mining company can legally operate on your land without your permission.
- **Community Land Consent:** If mining is on community land, there must be express community consent
- **Compulsory Acquisition Exception:** Foreign investors may seek compulsory land acquisition if private or community landowners withhold consent (Sec. 40), but this is a high legal bar
- **Right to Compensation:** Landowners are entitled to fair compensation for use of their land, disruption, and environmental damage
- **Right to Negotiate Terms:** Landowners can negotiate royalties, employment, and community benefit agreements before granting consent

**KEY STRATEGIC POINT:** The consent requirement is Valentine's most powerful legal lever. Without his consent, no one can legally mine on his family's land.

## 2. MINING LICENSES IN KENYA — TYPES, REQUIREMENTS, COSTS

### 2.1 Large-Scale Operations

| License Type | Purpose | Duration | Key Requirements |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reconnaissance Licence** | Initial exploration/survey | Up to 1 year | Application to Cabinet Secretary |
| **Prospecting Licence** | Detailed exploration | Up to 3 years (renewable) | Work programme, financial capacity |
| **Retention Licence** | Hold mineral rights while evaluating | Up to 5 years | Proven mineral deposit |
| **Mining Licence** | Full extraction operations | Up to 25 years (renewable) | EIA, financial capacity, state gets 10% free carried interest |

### 2.2 Small-Scale Operations

| License Type | Purpose | Key Requirements |
|---|---|---|
| **Prospecting Permit** | Small-scale exploration | Simpler application, lower fees |
| **Mining Permit** | Small-scale extraction | Environmental compliance, local regulations |

### 2.3 Licensing Exemptions

The Act provides important exemptions:

- **Artisanal Miners:** May be exempted from full licensing but must register and comply with regulations
- **Non-Commercial/Personal Use:** Small-scale extraction for personal use may not require a full mining licence but must comply with local rules
- **Mining on Private Land:** Certain exemptions may apply if mining is done with the landowner's consent and environmental standards are met
- **Government Institutions:** Exempt for research purposes
- **Foreign Investors:** May receive exemptions under bilateral/multilateral agreements

### 2.4 Application Process

All applications for mineral rights must be made through the **Online Mining Cadastre (OMC)** system at portal.miningcadastre.go.ke

### 2.5 State Participation

- The state is entitled to a **10% free carried interest** in large-scale mining operations (no financial contribution required from the state)
- The **National Mining Corporation** holds this interest on behalf of the state
- Holders of large mining licences with significant capital expenditure must **list at least 20% of their equity** on a local stock exchange within three years of production

## 3. HOW TO LEGALLY EXTRACT MINERALS FROM YOUR OWN LAND

### 3.1 Pathway for a Family Operation

**Option A: Artisanal Mining (Simplest Path)**
1. Register as an artisanal miner with the County Director of Mines
2. Obtain a mining permit (small-scale) through the Mining Cadastre
3. Comply with environmental and safety regulations
4. Sell minerals through licensed dealers only

**Option B: Small-Scale Mining Company**
1. Register a company (see Section 6)
2. Apply for a prospecting permit, then a mining permit
3. Obtain EIA licence from NEMA
4. Negotiate landowner consent agreements (if mining on family land, this is automatic)
5. Register as a mineral dealer

**Option C: Cooperative Structure (RECOMMENDED — see Section 5)**
1. Form a mining cooperative society
2. Register under the Co-operative Societies Act
3. Apply for mining permits through the cooperative
4. Benefits: shared costs, collective bargaining, legal protection

### 3.2 Key Legal Requirements

Before extracting any minerals, you must:
- ✅ Obtain the appropriate mining licence or permit
- ✅ Get an Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) licence from NEMA
- ✅ Have landowner consent (automatic if it's your own land)
- ✅ Register with the County Director of Mines
- ✅ Comply with safety standards
- ✅ Pay applicable royalties and taxes
- ✅ Sell only through licensed mineral dealers

## 4. COMMUNITY LAND ACT AND MINERAL RIGHTS

### 4.1 Community Land Act, 2016 (No. 27 of 2016)

The Community Land Act works alongside the Mining Act:

- **Community land** is defined as land lawfully registered in the name of group representatives, lawfully transferred to a specific community, or declared as community land by an Act of Parliament
- Minerals on community land still belong to the national government
- However, **community consent is required** before any mining activity on community land
- Communities have the right to negotiate benefit-sharing agreements
- Community land includes land traditionally occupied by hunter-gatherer communities, lawfully held by communities identified by law, and land declared as community land

### 4.2 What This Means for Community Ownership

- Communities **cannot own minerals** but can control **access** to their land
- Communities can negotiate for:
  - Royalty sharing
  - Employment quotas
  - Community development agreements
  - Infrastructure development
  - Environmental protection guarantees

## 5. KENYAN MINING COOPERATIVES — LEGAL STRUCTURE & REGISTRATION

### 5.1 Why a Cooperative is the Best Structure

A mining cooperative is the **strongest legal structure** for a family/community mining operation because:
- **Collective bargaining power** against larger companies
- **Shared costs** for permits, equipment, and legal fees
- **Legal personality** — can enter contracts, sue, and be sued
- **Limited liability** for members
- **Government support** — Kenya's cooperative movement is well-established
- **Access to funding** through cooperative development programs
- **Protection against exploitation** by middlemen and foreign operators

### 5.2 Legal Framework

Mining cooperatives in Kenya are governed by:
- **Co-operative Societies Act, Cap 490** (revised 2012)
- **Mining Act, 2016** (artisanal mining provisions)
- **County Government regulations**

### 5.3 How to Register a Mining Cooperative

**Step 1: Form a Founding Group**
- Minimum **10 members** (can be family members and trusted community members)
- Each member must be at least 18 years old
- Members must share a common bond (same area, same mineral interest)

**Step 2: Draft the By-Laws**
- Name of the cooperative
- Objectives (mining, processing, sale of minerals)
- Membership rules
- Share capital structure
- Management structure (committee members)
- Distribution of profits
- Dispute resolution mechanisms

**Step 3: Hold a Founding Meeting**
- All founding members must attend
- Elect interim officials (Chairman, Secretary, Treasurer)
- Adopt the by-laws
- Set share capital (minimum KES 1,000 per member recommended)

**Step 4: Register with the Commissioner of Co-operatives**
- Submit: Application form, by-laws, minutes of founding meeting, list of members
- Registration fee: approximately KES 500-2,000
- Processing time: 2-4 weeks
- Registration is done at the County Co-operative Officer's office

**Step 5: Post-Registration Requirements**
- Open a bank account in the cooperative's name
- Register with KRA for tax purposes
- Apply for mining permits through the Mining Cadastre
- Register with the County Director of Mines

### 5.4 Cooperative Management Structure

```
Annual General Meeting (AGM)
    ↓
Management Committee (elected)
    ├── Chairman
    ├── Vice Chairman
    ├── Secretary
    ├── Treasurer
    └── Committee Members (3-5)
    ↓
Sub-Committees (optional)
    ├── Mining Operations
    ├── Finance & Marketing
    └── Health, Safety & Environment
```

### 5.5 Successful Cooperative Model

The most successful mining cooperatives in Kenya:
- Have **clear governance structures** with regular audits
- Maintain **transparent financial records**
- Invest in **member training** on safe mining practices
- Establish **direct relationships** with licensed mineral dealers (cutting out exploitative middlemen)
- Create **community development funds** from a portion of profits
- Lobby collectively for better prices and terms

## 6. HOW TO FORM A MINING COMPANY IN KENYA

### 6.1 Company Types

| Type | Min. Directors | Min. Shareholders | Liability | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Private Limited Company (Ltd)** | 1 | 1 | Limited to shares | Family business |
| **Public Limited Company (PLC)** | 2 | 7 | Limited to shares | Large operations, listing |
| **Company Limited by Guarantee** | 1 | 1 | Limited by guarantee | Non-profit/community |

### 6.2 Steps to Register a Mining Company

1. **Reserve Company Name** — via eCitizen portal (KES 150)
2. **Prepare Documents:**
   - Memorandum of Association
   - Articles of Association
   - Form CR1 (Application for registration)
   - Form CR2 (Particulars of directors/shareholders)
   - Form CR8 (Notice of registered office)
3. **Register with the Registrar of Companies** — online via eCitizen
4. **Registration Fee:** KES 10,000-25,000 depending on share capital
5. **Get KRA PIN** for the company
6. **Register for VAT** if turnover exceeds KES 5 million
7. **Apply for mining permits** through the Mining Cadastre

### 6.3 Recommended Structure for Valentine

**A dual structure is recommended:**
1. **Family Cooperative** — for artisanal/small-scale mining operations
2. **Private Limited Company** — for larger operations, partnerships with investors, or mineral trading

This allows flexibility: the cooperative handles day-to-day mining, while the company can enter joint ventures, attract investment, and handle export operations.

## 7. ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT ASSESSMENT (EIA) REQUIREMENTS

### 7.1 Legal Requirement

Under the **Environmental Management and Coordination Act (EMCA), 1999** (amended 2015):
- **All mining projects** require an EIA licence from NEMA (National Environment Management Authority)
- No mining licence can be issued without a valid EIA licence
- The EIA must be conducted by a NEMA-approved expert

### 7.2 EIA Process for Mining

1. **Screening:** Determine if the project requires a full EIA (mining always does)
2. **Scoping:** Identify key environmental issues to study
3. **Baseline Study:** Document existing environmental conditions
4. **Impact Assessment:** Predict and evaluate environmental impacts
5. **Mitigation Measures:** Propose measures to minimize negative impacts
6. **Public Participation:** Consult affected communities (mandatory)
7. **EIA Report:** Submit to NEMA for review
8. **NEMA Review:** 90-day review period
9. **EIA Licence:** Issued if approved, with conditions

### 7.3 EIA Costs

- **Small-scale mining:** KES 200,000 - 500,000
- **Medium-scale mining:** KES 500,000 - 2,000,000
- **Large-scale mining:** KES 2,000,000+
- Costs include consultant fees, laboratory analysis, public participation meetings

### 7.4 Key Environmental Requirements for Mining

- Water management and pollution prevention
- Air quality management (dust control)
- Noise management
- Waste management (tailings, hazardous materials)
- Land rehabilitation plan
- Biodiversity protection
- Community health and safety measures

### 7.5 Enforcement

- NEMA conducts regular inspections
- Non-compliance can result in fines up to **KES 2 million** or imprisonment
- The **National Environment Tribunal** hears appeals
- Recent case law (Base Titanium v Kiswili, 2025) confirmed that mining licences do not override environmental rights

## 8. KENYA'S MINERAL EXPORT REGULATIONS

### 8.1 Export Requirements

Under the Mining (Dealings in Minerals) Regulations, 2017:

- **Export Permit Required:** Any holder of a mineral right, mineral dealer licence, or diamond dealer's licence must apply to the **Director of Mines** for an export permit
- **Declaration at Entry Points:** Importers must declare minerals at points of entry
- **Record Keeping:** Complete and accurate records of all mineral transactions must be maintained

### 8.2 Export Process

1. Obtain a mineral dealer's licence (if trading, not mining)
2. Apply for export permit from the Director of Mines
3. Provide documentation: source of minerals, quantity, destination, buyer details
4. Pay applicable royalties and taxes
5. Customs clearance at port of exit

### 8.3 Preferential Terms

- The Act allows for **preferential export terms** under mineral agreements
- Potential **royalty reductions** or delayed payments for foreign investors dealing in strategic minerals
- The establishment of a **Minerals and Metal Commodity Exchange** aims to facilitate secure transactions

### 8.4 Import Duty Exemptions

- **Import duty exemptions** are available for large-scale mining equipment and machinery
- Designed to encourage foreign direct investment in the mining sector

### 8.5 Illegal Export Warning

**WARNING:** Migori County's gold generates an estimated **$37 million annually**, yet the county collects only **KES 3.2 million in royalties**. Much gold is smuggled to Uganda where it fetches nearly international prices. **Illegal export is a criminal offense.** Always use legal channels.

## 9. HOW FOREIGN COMPANIES (CHINESE) LEGALLY OPERATE IN KENYAN MINING

### 9.1 Legal Framework for Foreign Investors

Foreign companies can legally operate in Kenya's mining sector by:

1. **Incorporating a Kenyan subsidiary** (most common)
2. **Entering joint ventures** with local companies
3. **Obtaining mineral rights** through the Mining Cadastre

### 9.2 Requirements for Foreign Mining Companies

- Must register a company in Kenya (or partner with a registered Kenyan company)
- Must obtain all required mining licences and permits
- Must comply with **local equity participation** rules: holders of large mining licences must list at least 20% of equity on a local stock exchange within three years of production
- Must enter a **mineral agreement** with the government for large-scale operations
- Must comply with EIA requirements
- Must respect landowner consent requirements

### 9.3 State Participation

- The state gets **10% free carried interest** in all large-scale mining operations
- The **National Mining Corporation** is the state's investment arm

### 9.4 Problems with Chinese Mining Operations in Migori

Investigative reporting reveals serious issues:

- **H-Nuo Kenya Limited** was caught tunneling under private land without permission in Kitere village (February 2023)
- Companies have been accused of **stealing minerals** from beneath private land
- **Environmental devastation:** dried-up wells, cracked farmland, destroyed aquifers
- **Inadequate compensation** offered to affected landowners
- Companies sometimes **pack up and leave** after extracting minerals, leaving environmental damage

**LESSON FOR VALENTINE:** Foreign companies must get your **express consent** before mining on your land. If they tunnel under your property without permission, that is **illegal** and you have grounds for legal action and compensation.

### 9.5 Bilateral Investment Treaties

- Kenya has bilateral investment treaties with China
- These provide protections for Chinese investors but also create frameworks for dispute resolution
- The **Kenya National Chamber of Commerce** actively courts Chinese investment

## 10. LEGAL PROTECTIONS FOR LOCAL LANDOWNERS

### 10.1 Constitutional Protections

The Constitution of Kenya 2010 provides:

- **Article 60:** Equitable access to land and natural resources
- **Article 62:** Public land includes mineral rights, but private land rights are protected
- **Article 66:** Regulation of land use and property
- **Article 69:** Environmental obligations including sustainable resource management
- **Article 40:** Right to property (cannot be deprived without compensation)
- **Article 35:** Right to access information (about mining operations)

### 10.2 Mining Act Protections

- **Consent requirement** for mining on private/community land
- **Compensation rights** for land use and damage
- **Environmental rehabilitation** obligations for mining companies
- **Community development agreements** can be mandated
- **Public participation** requirements in EIA process

### 10.3 Remedies for Landowners

If your rights are violated:

1. **Report to the County Director of Mines** — unauthorized mining
2. **File complaint with NEMA** — environmental damage
3. **Petition the Cabinet Secretary for Mining** — licence violations
4. **File suit in the Environment and Land Court** — constitutional rights violations
5. **Report to police** — criminal trespass, theft of minerals
6. **Seek compensation** through negotiation, mediation, or court
7. **Engage the National Land Commission** — land rights violations

### 10.4 Recent Legal Precedent

**Base Titanium v Kiswili (2025):** The court confirmed that mining licences do not override environmental rights. Landowners can challenge mining operations that cause environmental harm, even if the mining company has a valid licence.

## 12. COUNTY vs NATIONAL GOVERNMENT MINING JURISDICTION

### 12.1 Division of Powers

Under Kenya's devolved system:

| Level | Jurisdiction |
|---|---|
| **National Government** | Mineral rights, licensing, royalties, policy, large-scale mining regulation, export permits |
| **County Government** | Land use planning, local permits, artisanal mining coordination, environmental management at local level |

### 12.2 Migori County Specifics

- The **County Director of Mines** handles local mining coordination
- County government can facilitate artisanal miner registration
- County collects some revenue from mining activities
- County government is responsible for local infrastructure supporting mining

### 12.3 Practical Implications

- **Mining licences** are issued by the **national government** (Cabinet Secretary)
- **Local permits** and coordination come from **county government**
- **EIA licences** are issued by **NEMA** (national)
- **Land issues** are handled by both levels depending on the nature

## 15. TAX IMPLICATIONS OF MINING IN KENYA

### 15.1 Key Taxes

| Tax | Rate | Applies To |
|---|---|---|
| **Royalty** | 1-5% of mineral value | All mining operations |
| **Corporate Income Tax** | 30% | Mining companies |
| **VAT** | 16% | Mining services and equipment |
| **Withholding Tax** | 5-20% | Payments to contractors |
| **Mining Lease Fee** | Varies by area | Annual lease payments |

### 15.2 Incentives

- **Import duty exemptions** on mining equipment for large-scale operations
- **Fiscal stability agreements** available for large investors (tax certainty for a defined period)
- **Preferential export terms** under mineral agreements
- **Royalty reductions** possible for strategic minerals or special circumstances

### 15.3 Small-Scale Miner Tax Position

- Artisanal miners may qualify for simplified tax regimes
- Cooperative societies may benefit from cooperative tax provisions
- **Always register with KRA** and maintain proper records to avoid penalties
